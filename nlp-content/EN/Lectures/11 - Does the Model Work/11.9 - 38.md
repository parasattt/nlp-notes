### Regression or classification?

Notebook 10 asked: is a star rating a number or a category? Measure both models with **both**
measures:

```python
reg_pred = final.predict(y_test_df["text"])
reg_rounded = np.clip(np.round(reg_pred), 1, 5)
true_stars = y_test_df["stars"].to_numpy()

for name, p in [("regression (Ridge), rounded", reg_rounded), ("classification (LogReg)", pred)]:
    print(f"{name:30}  exact {np.mean(p == true_stars):.3f}   within 1 star {np.mean(np.abs(p - true_stars) <= 1):.3f}"
          f"   MAE {mae(p, true_stars):.3f}")
```

**Saved output:**

```text
regression (Ridge), rounded     exact 0.426   within 1 star 0.905   MAE 0.673
classification (LogReg)         exact 0.534   within 1 star 0.900   MAE 0.610
```

On this data the classifier is exactly right far more often (0.53 against 0.43) and even has the
smaller MAE (0.61 against 0.67). Within one star they tie: about 90% for both. The regression model pulls its predictions toward
the middle (MSE punishes a large miss heavily, so predicting 3 is the "safe" choice), and rounding
those cautious predictions often lands one star short at the extremes.

There is no general rule: the right framing is the one that wins on a measure that matches what the
product needs. Here: if the site shows a star estimate, "within one star" may matter more than
"exactly right" - and then the two models are equal.

# Part 7. Checklist

Before trusting any model's number:

1. **Is there a baseline?** Always the average / always the most common class.
2. **Was the number measured on data the model did not train on?**
3. **Was anything chosen with the test set?** Hyperparameters, features, vocabulary, thresholds - all
   of these must be chosen on training data (validation or cross-validation).
4. **How big is the gap between training and test score?** A large gap = overfitting → more data,
   stronger regularisation, or a simpler model. Both bad = underfitting → better features or a more
   flexible model.
5. **Does the measure match the cost of mistakes?** Accuracy for balanced classes with equal costs;
   precision / recall / F1 otherwise; MAE in real units for regression.
6. **How many test examples?** With 30 test examples one mistake is 3.3 points; with 3 000 it is
   0.03.

These rules do not change when the model does. Every model later in the course - including neural
networks with millions of parameters, where overfitting is the main daily problem - is judged by
exactly this list.

# Recap

- A model is judged on data it has **not** seen: **generalisation**. A memoriser has perfect training
  error and baseline test error.
- **Underfitting**: too simple, training and test error both high. **Overfitting**: too flexible,
  training error far below test error. The **gap** measures overfitting.
- With more weights than examples, plain linear regression on words fits every training review
  exactly and does **worse than the baseline** on new ones (1.41 vs 1.17).
- **Regularisation** adds a **penalty** on large weights: loss = MSE + $\alpha \sum w^2$ (**Ridge**,
  **L2**). Too little → overfitting, too much → underfitting. `C` in logistic regression is the same
  thing inverted.
- Choose hyperparameters with a **validation set** or **cross-validation** on training data only.
  Using the test set for any choice is **data leakage**.
- For classifiers: the **confusion matrix** (TP, FP, FN, TN), **accuracy**, **precision** (are the
  alarms real?), **recall** (are the real cases caught?), **F1**. Accuracy misleads on **imbalanced**
  data.
- Star ratings: the 5-class classifier is exactly right 53% of the time, within one star 90%, and
  mostly confuses neighbouring ratings.
