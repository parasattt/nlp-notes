# Part 2. Too simple, too flexible: 8 points

Back to the translation agency from notebook 10. Real jobs are noisy: two texts of the same length
can take different times. Here are 8 past jobs to train on and 8 new jobs to test on, generated as
*10 minutes per 100 words + 2, plus random noise* - so we know the true pattern is a straight line.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
def make_jobs(n):
    x = np.sort(rng.uniform(1, 8, n))
    return x, 10 * x + 2 + rng.normal(0, 6, n)

x_tr, y_tr = make_jobs(8)
x_te, y_te = make_jobs(8)
print("train x:", x_tr.round(1)); print("train y:", y_tr.round(1))
```

**Saved output:**

```text
train x: [1.6 1.7 2.1 2.7 4.  4.4 5.1 6.6]
train y: [12.8 38.5 24.5 26.5 40.6 41.5 46.4 65.7]
```

Three models of increasing flexibility. A **polynomial** of degree $d$ is a weighted sum of powers of
$x$: $w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + \dots + w_d x^d + b$. Degree 1 is the straight line. Higher degrees can bend:
degree 3 can make two bends, degree 7 can bend six times. With 8 points, degree 7 has 8 parameters -
enough to pass **exactly** through every training point.

The number of parameters and the ways they can combine is often called the model's **capacity**:
how complicated a pattern it can represent.

```python
xs = np.linspace(0.8, 8.2, 300)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11, 3.3), sharey=True)
results = []
for ax, degree in zip(axes, [0, 1, 7]):
    coefs = np.polyfit(x_tr, y_tr, degree)          # least squares fit of a polynomial
    tr_err = mae(np.polyval(coefs, x_tr), y_tr)
    te_err = mae(np.polyval(coefs, x_te), y_te)
    results.append((degree, tr_err, te_err))
    ax.scatter(x_tr, y_tr, label="train", zorder=3)
    ax.scatter(x_te, y_te, marker="x", color="tab:red", label="test", zorder=3)
    ax.plot(xs, np.polyval(coefs, xs), color="black", lw=1)
    ax.set_ylim(-10, 110); ax.set_xlabel("words (hundreds)")
    ax.set_title(f"degree {degree}: train MAE {tr_err:.1f}, test MAE {te_err:.1f}", fontsize=9)
axes[0].set_ylabel("minutes"); axes[0].legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print(pd.DataFrame(results, columns=["degree", "train MAE", "test MAE"]).round(2).to_string(index=False))
```

**Saved output:**

```text
 degree  train MAE  test MAE
      0      11.86     16.42
      1       5.08      5.23
      7       0.00   7868.23
```

- **Degree 0** is a flat line: always the same answer, like the baseline. It is wrong on train and
  on test alike. A model too simple to capture the pattern is **underfitting**.
- **Degree 1**, the straight line, has errors on both sets of similar size - about the size of the
  noise. That is the best any model can do here.
- **Degree 7** passes through every training point: train MAE is practically zero. Between the points
  it swings wildly, and beyond the last training point it shoots off the chart. Two test jobs (101
  and 781 words) lie just outside the training range, and the curve's predictions for them are in
  the thousands of minutes - hence the test MAE of 7 868. A model that fits the noise of its
  training data instead of the pattern is **overfitting**.

The signs of each, which you can check on any model:

| | training error | test error |
|---|---|---|
| underfitting | high | high (similar) |
| good fit | low-ish | close to training error |
| overfitting | very low | much higher than training error |

The gap between training error and test error is the measure of overfitting.
