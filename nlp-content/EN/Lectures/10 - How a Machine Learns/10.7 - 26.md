The colours use `log10` of the loss (the number of digits, roughly) so that both the steep walls
and the flat valley floor are visible. The valley is long and narrow: many combinations of $w$ and
$b$ give almost the same loss, for example a slightly steeper line that starts lower. That is why
the last part of the walk, along the valley floor, takes most of the steps.

### 4.4 The learning rate matters

```python
for lr in [0.001, 0.02, 0.08, 0.09]:
    p = gradient_descent(lr=lr, steps=100)
    w, b = p[-1]
    print(f"learning rate {lr:5}:  after 100 steps  w = {w:10.2f}  b = {b:10.2f}  MSE = {mse(predict(x, w, b), y):12.2f}")
```

**Saved output:**

```text
learning rate 0.001:  after 100 steps  w =       8.87  b =       2.42  MSE =        10.77
learning rate  0.02:  after 100 steps  w =       9.87  b =       2.26  MSE =         0.60
learning rate  0.08:  after 100 steps  w =       9.98  b =       1.86  MSE =         0.56
learning rate  0.09:  after 100 steps  w = -1907179.67  b = -528259.11  MSE = 46335118730958.85
```

- **0.001**: too small. Every step is tiny; after 100 steps it is still far from the bottom.
- **0.02**: fine.
- **0.08**: the best of the four after 100 steps, but on the edge. Each step overshoots the bottom
  and lands on the other wall of the valley, a little lower each time, zig-zagging inward.
- **0.09**: too large. Each step overshoots so far that it lands **higher** on the other wall, and
  the next step even higher. The numbers explode. This is called **divergence**.

Choosing the learning rate is part of the work. Numbers like this, which you set *before* training
and which training does not change, are called **hyperparameters** - as opposed to the parameters
$w$ and $b$, which training finds. The `C` of logistic regression in week 5 was also a
hyperparameter.

### 4.5 What `sklearn` does

For linear regression with MSE, there is a direct formula for the bottom of the bowl (the method of
**least squares**), so `LinearRegression` does not need gradient descent at all:

```python
lin = LinearRegression().fit(x.reshape(-1, 1), y)
print(f"sklearn:          w = {lin.coef_[0]:.3f}   b = {lin.intercept_:.3f}")
print(f"gradient descent: w = {path[-1, 0]:.3f}   b = {path[-1, 1]:.3f}")
print()
print("quote for a 750-word text:", round(lin.predict([[7.5]])[0], 1), "minutes")
```

**Saved output:**

```text
sklearn:          w = 10.000   b = 1.800
gradient descent: w = 10.000   b = 1.801

quote for a 750-word text: 76.8 minutes
```

The same line. (`reshape(-1, 1)` turns the list of 5 numbers into a table with 5 rows and 1 column,
because `sklearn` always expects $X$ to be a table, even with one feature.)

Most models have no such formula - logistic regression from week 5 does not, and neither do neural
networks. For them gradient descent, as you wrote it above, **is** the training.
