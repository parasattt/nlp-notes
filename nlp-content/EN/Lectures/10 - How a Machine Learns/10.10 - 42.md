### One weight per word

Hand-picked lists miss most words. As in weeks 4 and 5, let every word be a feature: TF-IDF gives
about 18 000 columns, and the model learns one weight per word. We use `Ridge`, which is linear
regression with one addition that notebook 11 explains (the reason is important and has its own
section). For now: same model, same loss.

```python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(min_df=2)
T_train = tfidf.fit_transform(y_train_df["text"])
T_test = tfidf.transform(y_test_df["text"])
print("T_train shape:", T_train.shape)

words_model = Ridge(alpha=1.0).fit(T_train, y_train_df["stars"])
print(f"test MAE: {mae(words_model.predict(T_test), y_test_df['stars']):.3f}  "
      f"(four features 0.973, baseline 1.173)")
```

**Saved output:**

```text
T_train shape: (12000, 18468)
test MAE: 0.727  (four features 0.973, baseline 1.173)
```

0.73 stars off on average - better than four features, because the model can now use every word.
Which words did it find?

```python
weights = pd.Series(words_model.coef_, index=tfidf.get_feature_names_out()).sort_values()
print("lowers the rating :", ", ".join(weights.index[:12]))
print()
print("raises the rating :", ", ".join(weights.index[::-1][:12]))
```

**Saved output:**

```text
lowers the rating : worst, not, horrible, rude, disgusting, worse, sick, poisoning, bland, no, barely, disappointing

raises the rating : best, amazing, delicious, great, awesome, love, perfect, wonderful, phenomenal, incredible, favorite, excellent
```

Nobody gave the model a list of positive and negative words; it found them from 12 000 examples.
Note `not` and `no` among the strongest negative words, and `poisoning` and `sick`: a restaurant
review that mentions food poisoning is rarely a 5-star one.

Now some reviews of our own:

```python
mine = ["absolutely amazing, best pizza in town",
        "the food was ok but the service was slow",
        "never again. rude staff",
        "not bad at all"]
for text, pred in zip(mine, words_model.predict(tfidf.transform(mine))):
    print(f"{pred:5.2f} stars   {text}")
```

**Saved output:**

```text
 5.82 stars   absolutely amazing, best pizza in town
 1.12 stars   the food was ok but the service was slow
 0.45 stars   never again. rude staff
 0.86 stars   not bad at all
```

Two things to notice.

- **5.82 and 0.45 stars.** The model does not know that ratings stop at 1 and 5. A weighted sum can
  be any number; the limits exist only in our heads. If a real number range matters, you clip the
  prediction (`np.clip(pred, 1, 5)`) or use a model whose output is bounded - which is exactly what
  the sigmoid did for probabilities in week 5.
- **`not bad at all` gets 0.86 stars.** Both `not` and `bad` have negative weights, and a weighted
  sum can only add them up. A linear model cannot express "`not` reverses the next word". This
  limit is the reason the course later moves to models that combine features in more complex ways.
