## The negation trap



Before filtering meaning-sensitive text, check whether its important negations appear in the list.

```python
print("'not' in the stop list?    ", "not" in stop_en)
print("'no' in the stop list?     ", "no" in stop_en)
print("'never' in the stop list?  ", "never" in stop_en)
```


**Saved output:**

```text
'not' in the stop list?     True
'no' in the stop list?      True
'never' in the stop list?   False
```



Compare a positive sentence and its negative version after applying exactly the same stop word filter.

```python
a = "the product is good"
b = "the product is not good"

ta = [w for w in word_tokenize(a) if w not in stop_en]
tb = [w for w in word_tokenize(b) if w not in stop_en]

print("positive ->", ta)
print("negative ->", tb)
print("identical after stop word removal?", ta == tb)
```


**Saved output:**

```text
positive -> ['product', 'good']
negative -> ['product', 'good']
identical after stop word removal? True
```



These sentences have opposite meanings, but stop word removal turns them into identical token lists. A classifier receiving only these lists cannot tell them apart: the information has already been removed.

The lecture's rule is: removing stop words can help topic analysis and harm meaning-sensitive analysis. **Keep negations** when their meaning matters. The same issue appears again in [[EN/Lectures/6 - Turning Text into Features/6.8 - Stop Words and Negation|feature vectors]].



`NEGATIONS` lists forms to protect. Set subtraction removes them from the words to be filtered, so `not` remains in the negative sentence.

```python
NEGATIONS = {"not", "no", "never", "nor", "none", "cannot", "n't"}
stop_safe = stop_en - NEGATIONS

print("negative now ->", [w for w in word_tokenize(b) if w not in stop_safe])
```


**Saved output:**

```text
negative now -> ['product', 'not', 'good']
```
