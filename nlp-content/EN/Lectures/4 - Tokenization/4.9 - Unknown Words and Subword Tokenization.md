## Tokens smaller than words

A word vocabulary has a fixed size. When a model meets a word outside that vocabulary, it may have no separate representation for it. This is the **out-of-vocabulary** problem.



This fixed word vocabulary replaces any missing string with `<UNK>`. Compare `unhappiness` with the known related word `unhappy`.

```python
vocab = {"the", "cat", "sat", "on", "mat", "happy", "unhappy"}

sentence = "the unhappiness of the cat"
print([w if w in vocab else "<UNK>" for w in sentence.split()])
```


**Saved output:**

```text
['the', '<UNK>', '<UNK>', 'the', 'cat']
```



`unhappiness` becomes `<UNK>`, so this representation loses its identity even though the vocabulary contains the related word `unhappy`. `of` also becomes `<UNK>` in this example.

**Subword tokenization** keeps common words whole and splits less common words into frequent pieces. The lecture presents this as the modern solution to unknown words, with individual characters as a fallback. Its broad “nothing is ever unknown” wording is the lecture's claim; the examples below show the actual pieces produced by the named tokenizers.

`AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")` selects the named tokenizer. `.tokenize()` shows its subword pieces for four words.

```python
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

bert = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")

for w in ["tokenization", "unhappiness", "kazakhstan", "cat"]:
    print(f"{w:15} -> {bert.tokenize(w)}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
tokenization    -> ['token', '##ization']
unhappiness     -> ['un', '##ha', '##pp', '##iness']
kazakhstan      -> ['kazakhstan']
cat             -> ['cat']
```



`cat` stays whole. `tokenization` becomes `token` + `##ization`; `##` marks a piece that continues the previous token. `unhappiness` is represented by several known pieces, so the tokenizer does not need a whole-word vocabulary entry for it.
