## Check split stability and vocabulary coverage

The source describes an earlier version with only **sixteen** reviews. Changing `random_state` alone moved its accuracy from 0.17 to 0.67, often below guessing. The code was not the problem: there were too few examples for training and testing to share enough vocabulary. Many test words were unseen during training, and smoothing could push predictions toward the larger class.

The corpus was doubled to 32 reviews and written with more overlapping vocabulary. The next loop checks several different splits of this revised corpus. It creates a fresh vectorizer and model for every split, so each vocabulary is learned only from that split's training examples.



```python
for seed in [0, 1, 2, 3, 7, 42]:
    a, b, c, d = train_test_split(review_texts, review_labels,
                                  test_size=0.25, random_state=seed,
                                  stratify=review_labels)
    v = CountVectorizer()
    m = MultinomialNB().fit(v.fit_transform(a), c)
    print(f"random_state={seed:2}: {accuracy_score(d, m.predict(v.transform(b))):.3f}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
random_state= 0: 1.000
random_state= 1: 1.000
random_state= 2: 1.000
random_state= 3: 1.000
random_state= 7: 0.875
random_state=42: 0.875
```



The saved accuracies are 1.000 for seeds 0, 1, 2, and 3, and 0.875 for seeds 7 and 42. That is much more stable than the earlier sixteen-review version, although the test sets are still small.

Changing only the split should not change the conclusion wildly. Before reporting one accuracy, the source recommends checking three or four different `random_state` values. Large differences tell you that the reported number depends heavily on which examples happened to reach training and testing.
