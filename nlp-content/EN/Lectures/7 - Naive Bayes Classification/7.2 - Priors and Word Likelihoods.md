## Learn from a small labelled collection

The eight messages below are deliberately short and few in number. You can check every count and probability by hand. Four messages are labelled spam and four are labelled ham.



```python
train_texts = [
    "free money click now",
    "win free cash prize",
    "claim your free prize now",
    "urgent click to win money",
    "meeting moved to three tomorrow",
    "please send the report today",
    "lunch at three tomorrow",
    "the report is ready for review",
]
train_labels = ["spam", "spam", "spam", "spam", "ham", "ham", "ham", "ham"]

for t, l in zip(train_texts, train_labels):
    print(f"{l:5} | {t}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
spam  | free money click now
spam  | win free cash prize
spam  | claim your free prize now
spam  | urgent click to win money
ham   | meeting moved to three tomorrow
ham   | please send the report today
ham   | lunch at three tomorrow
ham   | the report is ready for review
```



## Priors, word likelihoods, and Bayes' rule

The question is: **given this message's words, is spam or ham more likely?** For words $w_1 \dots w_n$, we want to compare:

$$P(\text{spam} \mid w_1 \dots w_n) \quad \text{vs} \quad P(\text{ham} \mid w_1 \dots w_n)$$

The vertical bar means “given.” Directly counting these probabilities would require many labelled copies of this exact message. That is not practical. Bayes' rule lets us use smaller quantities that we can count:

$$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{words}) \;\propto\; P(\text{spam}) \times P(w_1 \mid \text{spam}) \times P(w_2 \mid \text{spam}) \times \dots$$

Read the right side step by step:

1. Start with $P(\text{spam})$, the **prior**: the fraction of training messages labelled spam, before reading any words. Here it is `4 / 8 = 0.5`; ham also has prior `0.5`.
2. For each word, find $P(w \mid \text{spam})$, its **word likelihood**: the word's count in spam divided by the total number of words in spam. These are word counts, not message counts.
3. Multiply the prior by all those word likelihoods. Do the same for ham, then compare the two scores.

The “naive” assumption is that words are independent from each other when the model makes its prediction. That assumption allows the multiplication. It is clearly not literally true: `prize` and `win` often appear together. The later examples show both why the method can still be useful and where this assumption causes trouble.

The next cells collect all spam and ham words separately, calculate the priors, count repeated words with `Counter`, and turn the counts into likelihoods. There are 18 spam word occurrences and 20 ham word occurrences. For example, `free` occurs three times in spam, so its spam likelihood is `3 / 18 = 0.1667`. `report` occurs twice in ham, so its ham likelihood is `2 / 20 = 0.1000`.

If the nested counting expressions are unfamiliar, review [[EN/Lectures/1 - Python Basics for Text/1.18 - Counting with Counter|counting with Counter]].



```python
spam_words = [w for t, l in zip(train_texts, train_labels) if l == "spam" for w in t.split()]
ham_words  = [w for t, l in zip(train_texts, train_labels) if l == "ham"  for w in t.split()]

p_spam = train_labels.count("spam") / len(train_labels)
p_ham  = train_labels.count("ham")  / len(train_labels)

print("P(spam) =", p_spam)
print("P(ham)  =", p_ham)
print()
print("total words in spam:", len(spam_words))
print("total words in ham :", len(ham_words))
```


**Saved output:**

```text
P(spam) = 0.5
P(ham)  = 0.5

total words in spam: 18
total words in ham : 20
```



```python
from collections import Counter

spam_counts = Counter(spam_words)
ham_counts  = Counter(ham_words)

print("in spam:", spam_counts.most_common(5))
print("in ham :", ham_counts.most_common(5))
```


**Saved output:**

```text
in spam: [('free', 3), ('money', 2), ('click', 2), ('now', 2), ('win', 2)]
in ham : [('three', 2), ('tomorrow', 2), ('the', 2), ('report', 2), ('meeting', 1)]
```



```python
def p_word_given(word, counts, total):
    return counts[word] / total

for w in ["free", "click", "report", "three"]:
    ps = p_word_given(w, spam_counts, len(spam_words))
    ph = p_word_given(w, ham_counts, len(ham_words))
    print(f"{w:8} P(w|spam)={ps:.4f}  P(w|ham)={ph:.4f}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
free     P(w|spam)=0.1667  P(w|ham)=0.0000
click    P(w|spam)=0.1111  P(w|ham)=0.0000
report   P(w|spam)=0.0000  P(w|ham)=0.1000
three    P(w|spam)=0.0000  P(w|ham)=0.1000
```



In this small training collection, `free` appears in spam and never in ham. `report` appears in ham and never in spam; `three` also appears only in ham.

These are observations about the training examples, not rules about all future messages. The next function uses the observed likelihoods to score a new message.
