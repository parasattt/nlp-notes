## Keep negation words when modelling sentiment

Do not remove stop words blindly in this example. The default English stop list includes `not`. Removing it changes `not worth the price` to `worth price`, losing the negative meaning.

Unigrams also separate words from their neighbours. Bigrams can keep pairs such as `not good` together as features, but only if the training data actually contains enough examples of those pairs.

See [[EN/Lectures/6 - Turning Text into Features/6.8 - Stop Words and Negation|Stop Words and Negation]] for the earlier lost-negation example, and [[EN/Lectures/6 - Turning Text into Features/6.10 - N-grams and Negation Features|N-grams and Negation Features]] for the representation choice.



`ngram_range=(1, 2)` keeps both single words and two-word sequences. The comparison uses the same full review corpus for both models, then prints both verdicts on the two test sentences.

```python
neg_test = ["this is not good at all", "this is good"]

uni = CountVectorizer()
uni_clf = MultinomialNB().fit(uni.fit_transform(review_texts), review_labels)

bi = CountVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2))
bi_clf = MultinomialNB().fit(bi.fit_transform(review_texts), review_labels)

for t in neg_test:
    u = uni_clf.predict(uni.transform([t]))[0]
    b = bi_clf.predict(bi.transform([t]))[0]
    print(f"unigram={u:8} bigram={b:8} | {t}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
unigram=negative bigram=negative | this is not good at all
unigram=positive bigram=positive | this is good
```



The saved unigram and bigram models both label `this is not good at all` negative and `this is good` positive. This does **not** demonstrate that bigrams are useless. The exact bigram `not good` appears zero times in the training reviews, so the bigram model cannot learn its meaning here.
