```python
# 5. A first crude sentiment feature
positive_words = ["good", "great", "excellent", "perfect", "best", "fantastic", "nice"]
negative_words = ["terrible", "broke", "avoid", "disappointing", "cheap", "overheats", "died"]

def score(text):
    words = text.split()
    pos = sum(1 for w in words if w.strip(".,!") in positive_words)
    neg = sum(1 for w in words if w.strip(".,!") in negative_words)
    return pos - neg

df["sentiment_score"] = df["clean"].apply(score)

df[["review", "rating", "sentiment_score"]].sort_values("sentiment_score")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
                                                      review  rating  sentiment_score
1      Screen died after three weeks. Support was unhelpful.       1               -1
3             Battery drains far too quickly, disappointing.       2               -1
5                 Does the job but the keyboard feels cheap.       3               -1
7                    Stopped charging within a month. Avoid.       1               -1
9        Overheats under any real load. Not worth the money.       2               -1
10            It is fine. Nothing special, nothing terrible.       3               -1
0   Excellent laptop, fast and quiet. Battery lasts all day.       5                1
2                Good phone for the price, camera is decent.       4                1
4          Perfect for reading and taking notes. Very light.       5                1
8         Nice tablet overall, though the speakers are weak.       4                1
11     Fantastic build quality and the display is beautiful.       5                1
6     Best phone I have owned. Fast, great screen, worth it.       5                2
```

`score()` splits a cleaned review into tokens, strips `.,!` from each token's ends for matching, counts positive and negative word-list matches, and returns **positive matches minus negative matches**. A positive score indicates more positive-list matches; a negative score indicates more negative-list matches.

Most saved scores are `1` or `-1`; the review containing both `Best` and `great` scores `2`. The neutral-sounding review containing `"Nothing ... terrible"` receives `-1`, illustrating that counting words alone does not understand their context.



```python
# 6. Does our crude score agree with the actual star rating?
df[["rating", "sentiment_score"]].corr().round(2)
```


**Saved output:**

```text
                 rating  sentiment_score
rating             1.00             0.89
sentiment_score    0.89             1.00
```

`.corr()` compares the numeric columns. The off-diagonal value **0.89** is their saved correlation, indicating that higher word-list scores tend to appear with higher star ratings in this tiny dataset. The diagonal values are `1.00` because each column is compared with itself. This is a correlation result, not a held-out accuracy score.



The saved correlation between the word-list score and rating is about **0.89**. That looks encouraging, but it uses only twelve reviews, and the word lists were chosen after looking at those same reviews. This makes the result optimistic, like grading an exam using answers you already saw. Expect weaker performance on unseen reviews.

This is still a useful **baseline**: a simple method against which to compare a more complex model. Build a simple baseline early so that you can tell how much a learned model adds. The source warns that a neural network beating it by only two points would not automatically be an impressive result.

The word lists cannot reliably handle:

- Negation, such as `"not good"`.
- Sarcasm.
- Different meanings of a word, such as `"broke a record"`.
- Words that were never added to the lists.

These limitations help explain the move to learned models, including [[EN/Lectures/7 - Naive Bayes Classification/7.0 - Overview|Naive Bayes]] and [[EN/Lectures/9 - Logistic Regression/9.0 - Overview|logistic regression]].
