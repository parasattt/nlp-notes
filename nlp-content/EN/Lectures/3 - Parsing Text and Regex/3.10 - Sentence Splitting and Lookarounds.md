## Split text into sentences

Some tasks need sentences rather than whole documents. Splitting at every period seems simple, but the same character also appears inside abbreviations and numbers.

### First, split at every period



```python
para = "Dr. Smith arrived at 3 p.m. He was late. The meeting cost $1.5 million. Everyone left."

print(para.split("."))
```


**Saved output:**

```text
['Dr', ' Smith arrived at 3 p', 'm', ' He was late', ' The meeting cost $1', '5 million', ' Everyone left', '']
```



The saved split breaks `Dr.`, `p.m.`, and `$1.5`, even though their periods are not sentence endings. This is why a period alone is an ambiguous sentence-boundary signal.

### Add conditions with regex

The next pattern requires punctuation followed by whitespace and a capital letter.



```python
# Split on . ! or ? followed by whitespace and a capital letter
sentences = re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+(?=[A-Z])", para)
for s in sentences:
    print("-", s)
```


**Saved output:**

```text
- Dr.
- Smith arrived at 3 p.m.
- He was late.
- The meeting cost $1.5 million.
- Everyone left.
```



`(?<=...)` is a **lookbehind** and `(?=...)` a **lookahead**. They check surrounding characters without including those characters in the matched separator.

The pattern splits at whitespace that follows `.`, `!`, or `?` and comes before a capital letter. This improves the example, but it still splits `Dr. Smith`, because `Smith` starts with a capital.

The lesson is that regex has limits. Sentence boundaries depend on abbreviation knowledge; tools such as spaCy and NLTK's `punkt` use trained information to handle these cases. Knowing when to switch tools is part of using regex well.
