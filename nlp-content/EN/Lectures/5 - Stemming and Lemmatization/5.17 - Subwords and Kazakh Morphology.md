The lecture interprets this as the correct morpheme split, learned from text statistics. The common strings `мектепте` and `қала` stay whole in the saved output.

Other results show why this is not a morphology analyser:

- `достарыма` becomes `до` + `стары` + `ма`, while the lecture gives the morphemes as `дос` + `тар` + `ым` + `а`.
- `дүкеннен` becomes `дүк` + `ен` + `нен`: it finds the ablative suffix but cuts the root in half.

Subword tokenizers find **frequent character sequences**. Repeated Kazakh suffixes sometimes make these sequences match morphemes, but the output does not tell us which splits follow grammar.

The lecture argues that subwords are useful for languages with fewer NLP resources and many suffix combinations because words can still be represented as pieces without expert-written rules. It describes this as never failing outright or producing `<UNK>`; keep that as the source's broad claim, alongside the actual saved examples. Our rule-based stemmer leaves `достарыма` untouched, whereas the tokenizer still produces pieces.

The pieces are not lemmas: `▁кітап` is not a dictionary headword. For counting mentions of books, the lecture prefers the stemmer. The task determines which tool is useful.
