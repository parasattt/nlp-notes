## Search through typos with character n-grams

Three reviews contain misspellings of `battery`: `batery` or `battry`. First try the correctly spelled query with word features to see which reviews become invisible.



```python
print("QUERY: battery")
search("battery", k=6)
```


**Saved output:**

```text
QUERY: battery
  0.453  [negative]  disappointed the battery died within a week
  0.384  [positive]  very satisfied the battery lasts all day highly recommend
  0.000  [negative]  I would NOT recommend this to anyone
  0.000  [negative]  Lasted less than a month. Meh.
  0.000  [negative]  Not as good as the reviews suggest
  0.000  [negative]  It works but the noise is unbearable
```



Only two results have nonzero similarity. The misspelled reviews get no match for `battery`, because a word vectorizer treats `batery` as a separate feature, unrelated to `battery`.

Instead of using whole words, we can represent overlapping **character n-grams**: short pieces of letters. Then two nearly identical spellings can share features.



```python
demo = TfidfVectorizer(analyzer="char_wb", ngram_range=(3, 3))
demo.fit(["battery", "batery"])
print(demo.get_feature_names_out())
```


**Saved output:**

```text
[' ba' 'ate' 'att' 'bat' 'ery' 'ry ' 'ter' 'tte']
```



`analyzer="char_wb"` builds character n-grams inside word boundaries, padding each word with a space at each end. With `ngram_range=(3, 3)`, every feature is a three-character piece.

`battery` and `batery` share many pieces, including ` ba`, `bat`, `ter`, `ery`, and `ry `. They can therefore have a nonzero similarity without a spell checker. The next search uses lengths 3 through 5, rather than only length 3.



`ngram_range=(3, 5)` creates character pieces of lengths 3, 4, and 5. The query and reviews use the same fitted character vocabulary; only their feature representation changes.

```python
char_tv = TfidfVectorizer(analyzer="char_wb", ngram_range=(3, 5))
char_matrix = char_tv.fit_transform(reviews)

scores = cosine_similarity(char_tv.transform(["battery"]), char_matrix)[0]
print("QUERY: battery   (character n-grams)")
for i in scores.argsort()[::-1][:6]:
    print(f"  {scores[i]:.3f}  [{review_labels[i]}]  {reviews[i]}")
print()
print("features:", len(tv_reviews.get_feature_names_out()), "(words) vs",
      len(char_tv.get_feature_names_out()), "(character n-grams)")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
QUERY: battery   (character n-grams)
  0.449  [negative]  disappointed the battery died within a week
  0.415  [positive]  very satisfied the battery lasts all day highly recommend
  0.172  [positive]  The batery easily lasts 2 days with heavy use
  0.162  [negative]  I wanted to love it but the batery stopped charging after a month
  0.136  [negative]  The battry drains overnight even when switched off
  0.068  [negative]  Expected much better from this brand

features: 376 (words) vs 3950 (character n-grams)
```



All three typo reviews now appear below the two correctly spelled battery reviews. Character overlap can also help with `recieved`/`received`, `colour`/`color`, and forms such as `charge`, `charging`, and `charger`.

The saved feature counts show one cost: 376 word features become 3,950 character features, roughly ten times as many. Another cost is that similar spelling does not always mean similar meaning. The next comparison tests that directly.



```python
pairs = [("happy", "unhappy"), ("the seller was helpful", "the seller was unhelpful")]

for p in pairs:
    w = TfidfVectorizer().fit_transform(p)
    c = TfidfVectorizer(analyzer="char_wb", ngram_range=(3, 5)).fit_transform(p)
    print(f"{str(p):52} words {cosine_similarity(w)[0, 1]:.3f}   characters {cosine_similarity(c)[0, 1]:.3f}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
('happy', 'unhappy')                                 words 0.000   characters 0.450
('the seller was helpful', 'the seller was unhelpful') words 0.603   characters 0.785
```



`happy` and `unhappy` have opposite meanings but share most of their letters. Their word-based similarity is 0, while character n-grams give 0.450. The seller sentences rise from 0.603 with words to 0.785 with characters.

The feature overlap that rescues a typo also makes opposites look more alike. This is another form of the negation problem. A stop list can delete the separate word `not`; character features can blur the `un` at the beginning of a word. Every representation makes some inputs look similar, so inspect whether its similarities fit the task.
