## Reduce common-word weights without deleting words

IDF is learned from the review corpus. Sorting `idf_` puts the words found in the most reviews at the low end. This lets us inspect which words the representation is weakening.



```python
idf_table = pd.Series(tv_reviews.idf_, index=tv_reviews.get_feature_names_out()).sort_values()

print("lowest idf (most common):")
print(idf_table.head(8).round(2))
print()
print("highest idf:", round(idf_table.max(), 2), "- shared by every word that appears only once")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
lowest idf (most common):
the        1.79
and        2.18
it         2.36
is         2.85
quality    2.96
not        3.09
this       3.16
product    3.16
dtype: float64

highest idf: 5.1 - shared by every word that appears only once
```



The lowest-IDF words look like a stop list: `the`, `and`, `it`, and `is`. The key difference is that TF-IDF **reduces their weights rather than removing them**.

`not` is sixth in the saved list. Its IDF is about 3.1, compared with a maximum of 5.1 for words seen once. It remains available for a classifier to use. In Lecture 6, `stop_words="english"` removed `not` and made an opposite pair of reviews identical. Keeping stop words with TF-IDF avoids that particular deletion.

The data boundary depends on the task. In **search**, the stored document corpus is what you want to search, so learning IDF from all those documents is appropriate. In **classification**, the test documents must remain unseen: fit TF-IDF only on training documents, just as with `CountVectorizer` in Lecture 7.
