## Turn document frequency into inverse document frequency

`the` appears in all 3 documents, `battery` appears in 2, and every other vocabulary word appears in 1. A word found in fewer documents is more useful for distinguishing one document from another.

**Inverse document frequency**, or IDF, gives a larger weight to those less common words:

$$\text{idf}(w) = \log \frac{N}{\text{df}(w)}$$

- `w` is the word whose weight we want.
- $N$ is the total number of documents.
- `df(w)` is the number of documents containing that word.
- `N / df(w)` increases as the word becomes rarer.
- The logarithm compresses the size of that ratio while keeping rarer words above common ones.

### Why use a logarithm?

The next table imagines a shop with 1,000 reviews. Compare the raw ratio with its natural logarithm for several document frequencies.



```python
N_shop = 1000
table = pd.DataFrame({"df": [1, 10, 100, 500, 1000]})
table["N / df"] = N_shop / table["df"]
table["log(N / df)"] = np.log(table["N / df"]).round(2)
print(table.to_string(index=False))
```


**Saved output:**

```text
  df  N / df  log(N / df)
   1  1000.0         6.91
  10   100.0         4.61
 100    10.0         2.30
 500     2.0         0.69
1000     1.0         0.00
```



A word in one of the 1,000 reviews has `N / df = 1000`; a word in ten reviews has `N / df = 100`. The ratio is **10 to 1**. The original paragraph calls this “100 times rarer” and “100 times the weight,” but its own table shows a factor of ten.

Without the log, a one-off word or typo such as `batery` could get a very large rarity weight. The log keeps the ordering while reducing the gap: `1000` becomes about `6.9`, and `100` becomes about `4.6`.

Think of IDF as measuring surprise. Seeing `the` gives almost no information about which review you are reading. Seeing a distinctive word such as `cracked` gives much more. The saved plot shows IDF falling as document frequency rises.



The plot uses a logarithmic document-frequency axis to show rare and common words together. The annotations place a typo, `cracked`, `is`, and `the` at example document frequencies.

```python
df_range = np.arange(1, N_shop + 1)

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3.2))
plt.plot(df_range, np.log(N_shop / df_range))
for d, label in [(1, "a typo"), (10, "'cracked'"), (500, "'is'"), (1000, "'the'")]:
    plt.scatter(d, np.log(N_shop / d), zorder=3)
    plt.annotate(label, (d, np.log(N_shop / d)), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(6, 6))
plt.xscale("log")
plt.ylim(-0.5, 8)
plt.xlabel("document frequency (log scale)")
plt.ylabel("idf")
plt.title("IDF falls as a word gets more common (N = 1000)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```
