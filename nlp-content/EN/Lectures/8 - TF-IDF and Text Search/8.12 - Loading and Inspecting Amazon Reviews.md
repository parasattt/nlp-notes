## Load a larger, real review corpus

The same method can search more than the 120 teaching reviews. The source uses **Amazon Reviews Polarity** (Zhang, Zhao and LeCun, 2015), collected from Amazon product pages. One- and two-star reviews are labelled negative; four- and five-star reviews are labelled positive.

The code downloads the dataset from the supplied Hugging Face URL, samples 20,000 rows with `random_state=0`, joins title and content into one text column, and converts the numeric label to a sentiment name. The notebook describes the download as taking about 20 seconds in Colab; that is source context, not a timing measured during this conversion.



```python
url = ("https://huggingface.co/datasets/fancyzhx/amazon_polarity/resolve/main/"
       "amazon_polarity/test-00000-of-00001.parquet")

amazon = pd.read_parquet(url).sample(20000, random_state=0).reset_index(drop=True)
amazon["text"] = amazon["title"] + ". " + amazon["content"]
amazon["sentiment"] = np.where(amazon["label"] == 1, "positive", "negative")

print(amazon.shape)
print(amazon["sentiment"].value_counts().to_dict())
print()
for t in amazon["text"].head(3):
    print("-", t[:160], "...")
```

The saved sample has 20,000 rows, with 10,074 negative and 9,926 positive reviews. The displayed examples keep their original spelling, capitals, and joined titles.

The reviews cover books, music, kitchen tools, phone cases, and more. They are longer than the teaching examples, as the saved histogram shows. The dashed line marks the teaching corpus's average length; the Amazon corpus has a saved median of 70 words.



The histogram counts words using whitespace splitting. It compares the distribution of real review lengths with a vertical reference for the mean length of the 120 teaching reviews.

```python
lengths = amazon["text"].str.split().str.len()

```
