### Simplify the context with a Markov assumption

The formula just introduced is the **chain rule**. Its later factors can have very long conditions, such as the exact previous nineteen words. Those full histories are too rare to estimate directly from counts.

A **bigram model** makes this approximation:

$P(w_i \mid \text{everything before}) \approx P(w_i \mid w_{i-1})$.

It keeps only the previous word. This is the **Markov assumption**. The assumption loses information: *I would not* tells more about the next word than *not* alone. A **trigram model** keeps the previous two words instead.

The code below multiplies the bigram probabilities, including the start/end markers, to score a sentence.



`sentence_prob` starts at 1 and multiplies each transition probability. Its argument `p` lets the same scoring loop use either raw or smoothed estimates later.

```python
def sentence_prob(sentence, p=p_next):
    words = add_marks(sentence)
    prob = 1.0
    for prev, word in zip(words, words[1:]):
        prob *= p(word, prev)
    return prob

for s in ["thank you for contacting us", "thank you for your order", "your order has shipped"]:
    print(f"{sentence_prob(s):.3f}   {s}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
0.200   thank you for contacting us
0.100   thank you for your order
0.100   your order has shipped
```



The first saved score is 0.2, matching the hand calculation. Such scores can compare which of two draft replies better resembles the team's usual wording.
