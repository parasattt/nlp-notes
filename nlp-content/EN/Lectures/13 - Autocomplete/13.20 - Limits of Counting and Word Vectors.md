### Counts do not share knowledge between related words

The next experiment compares `pizza`, `burrito`, `sushi`, and `pho`: their training frequencies, counts before *was delicious*, and the probability assigned to `delicious`.



```python
for w in ["pizza", "burrito", "sushi", "pho"]:
    print(f"{w:8} appears {uni[w]:5} times   'the {w} was delicious' seen {tri[(w, 'was', 'delicious')]:2} times"
          f"   P(delicious | {w} was) = {p_interp('delicious', w, 'was'):.4f}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
pizza    appears  4765 times   'the pizza was delicious' seen 12 times   P(delicious | pizza was) = 0.0221
burrito  appears   785 times   'the burrito was delicious' seen  0 times   P(delicious | burrito was) = 0.0025
sushi    appears  3143 times   'the sushi was delicious' seen  4 times   P(delicious | sushi was) = 0.0171
pho      appears   734 times   'the pho was delicious' seen  1 times   P(delicious | pho was) = 0.0178
```



The word sequence *pizza was delicious* was observed twelve times, while *burrito was delicious* was not observed. The interpolated probabilities for `delicious` are **0.0221 after `pizza was`** and **0.0025 after `burrito was`**, almost a ninefold difference.

A person can use the knowledge that pizza, burrito, and pho are all foods. A count table treats their strings as separate dictionary keys: learning about `pizza` does not directly help with `burrito`, just as it does not directly help with `parking`.

This is the earlier `sofa`/`couch` problem again. The lecture proposes **word vectors**, where related foods can be close together. It connects this to neural language models, which begin by representing words with vectors.

For how such vectors are learned, see [[EN/Lectures/12 - Smart Search with Word Vectors/12.7 - Word2Vec Training Pairs and Pair Scores|Word2Vec pair scoring]] and [[EN/Lectures/12 - Smart Search with Word Vectors/12.9 - Gradient Updates and Toy Word2Vec|its gradient updates]].
