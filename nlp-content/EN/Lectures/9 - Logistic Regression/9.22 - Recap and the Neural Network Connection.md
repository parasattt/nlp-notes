## Recap

- Logistic regression multiplies features by learned weights, adds a bias, and uses sigmoid to get a probability: $\sigma(w \cdot x + b)$. The bias $b$ sets the starting preference before any word contributes.
- Gradient descent learns weights by reducing log loss. Confident mistakes cost much more than uncertain predictions, like the forecaster who promised “99% sun.” The two-feature plot shows the updates; the full model uses the same idea and closely matches `sklearn`.
- Falling training loss does not guarantee improving test accuracy. The saved training curves show overfitting; with 30 test cases, one review is 3.3 percentage points.
- A Pipeline fits its vectorizer only on training data and uses the same representation at prediction time.
- LR does not beat NB on the 120 teaching reviews. The stored large-data results show LR ahead on 20,000 real reviews, illustrating the source's “NB learns quickly; LR benefits from more data” comparison. The large-data section also retains its saved SSL and `NameError` failures, so these outputs are not claimed as a fresh verified run.
- Useful outputs include probabilities, a threshold chosen for the task's error costs, and a readable signed coefficient per word.
- Smaller `C` means stronger regularisation and smaller weights. In this small experiment it mainly changes confidence, with little change in test accuracy.
- Some examples defeat every setup because vocabulary coverage is poor; others need information lost by bag-of-words features, including order, negation scope, actions such as returning a product, and discarded emoji.

## The connection to neural networks

Look again at the model formula:

$$\sigma(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_n x_n + b)$$

It has inputs, one weight per input, a sum, a bias, and a squashing function. This is **one artificial neuron**.

The next lecture connects many such neurons, feeding some outputs into other neurons. You will calculate a small network's output by hand, as with Naive Bayes and logistic regression. The weighted sum, sigmoid, log loss, and gradient-descent loop are already familiar pieces.
