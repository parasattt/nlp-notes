## Read signed coefficients

Naive Bayes keeps a word likelihood for each class. Logistic regression keeps **one signed weight per word**. In this binary model, a positive coefficient pushes toward positive, a negative coefficient pushes toward negative, and a larger absolute value means a stronger push for a given feature value.

The next cell extracts the classifier and fitted vectorizer from the pipeline, aligns coefficients with word names, and sorts them. These are the coefficients of the model fitted on all 120 teaching reviews, rather than the earlier model trained on 90.



```python
lr_step = lr_fit.named_steps["logisticregression"]
tf_step = lr_fit.named_steps["tfidfvectorizer"]

coefs = pd.Series(lr_step.coef_[0], index=tf_step.get_feature_names_out()).sort_values()

print("pushes toward NEGATIVE:")
print(coefs.head(8).round(2).to_string())
print()
print("pushes toward POSITIVE:")
print(coefs.tail(8)[::-1].round(2).to_string())
```

The class names are sorted alphabetically, so `positive` is class 1 and positive weights favour it.

The table makes suspicious patterns easy to see:

- `the` is the second strongest negative feature. Complaints often name an object, as in `the item`, `the battery`, or `the lid`. Is this a broad language pattern, or just a habit in these examples?
- `for` is the strongest positive feature, from phrases such as `for the price`, `for my parents`, and `perfect for travelling`.

With only 120 reviews, the coefficient table cannot establish whether these are useful patterns or coincidences. Naive Bayes can also be inspected for such words, but LR expresses the direction directly as one signed number. The large-data example later compares both suspicious words with real reviews.
