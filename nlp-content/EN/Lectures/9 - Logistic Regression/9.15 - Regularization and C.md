## Regularisation limits large weights

The hand-written training loop could keep increasing its weights and becoming more certain about the 90 training reviews. The library adds **regularisation** by default: a penalty on large weights.

Think of it as a cost for using large coefficients. Reducing training error still matters, but a large weight must earn enough benefit to justify that extra cost. This helps limit how strongly the model fits a small training set.

`C` controls how much freedom the weights have. **Smaller `C` means a stronger penalty and smaller weights. Larger `C` means a weaker penalty and more freedom for large weights.** Its direction is easy to mix up, so read the saved experiment with that relationship in mind.

The loop below keeps the same training and test split and varies only `C`. It compares training accuracy, test accuracy, the largest absolute weight, and the range of prediction confidence.



```python
print("   C     | train acc | test acc | largest weight | confidence range on test")
for C in [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000]:
    m = make_pipeline(TfidfVectorizer(), LogisticRegression(C=C, max_iter=5000))
    m.fit(tr_txt, y_tr)
    conf = m.predict_proba(te_txt).max(axis=1)
    biggest = np.abs(m.named_steps["logisticregression"].coef_).max()
    print(f" {C:7} |   {m.score(tr_txt, y_tr):.2f}    |   {m.score(te_txt, y_te):.2f}   |"
          f"     {biggest:5.2f}      |  {conf.min():.2f} - {conf.max():.2f}")
```

Test accuracy barely changes: it is 0.57 or 0.60, a difference of one review in this 30-review test set. Confidence changes much more.

At `C=0.01`, the largest weight is only 0.01 and confidence rounds to 0.50 throughout the test set. The model has very little preference even though many tiny differences still produce labels. At `C=1000`, the largest weight is 8.35 and confidence reaches a rounded 1.00, without better test accuracy.

For this small-data example, `C` mostly changes how confident the model becomes. The default `C=1` gives cautious probabilities, consistent with the earlier out-of-fold confidence range. With a larger dataset, the source says `C` can affect accuracy too and is normally chosen through cross-validation.

The next plot tracks individual weights as `C` changes, showing which coefficients grow in each direction.



`np.logspace(-2, 3, 21)` tries 21 `C` values from 0.01 to 1,000. A fresh pipeline is fitted at each setting, and six named word coefficients are recorded for the saved path plot.

```python
C_values = np.logspace(-2, 3, 21)
watch = ["not", "after", "broke", "the", "for", "great"]

paths = {word: [] for word in watch}
for C in C_values:
    m = make_pipeline(TfidfVectorizer(), LogisticRegression(C=C, max_iter=5000)).fit(tr_txt, y_tr)
    weights = pd.Series(m.named_steps["logisticregression"].coef_[0],
                        index=m.named_steps["tfidfvectorizer"].get_feature_names_out())
    for word in watch:
        paths[word].append(weights.get(word, 0.0))

plt.figure(figsize=(6.5, 3.6))
for word in watch:
    plt.plot(C_values, paths[word], label=word)
plt.axhline(0, color="grey", lw=0.8)
plt.axvline(1, color="black", lw=0.8, ls="--")
plt.text(1.1, plt.ylim()[0] * 0.9, "default C=1", fontsize=8)
plt.xscale("log")
plt.xlabel("C  (left: heavy penalty, right: light penalty)")
plt.ylabel("weight")
plt.title("Regularisation shrinks every weight toward 0")
plt.legend(fontsize=8, ncol=2)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

At the left of the saved plot, small `C` strongly shrinks every weight toward zero. As `C` increases to the right, the weights spread out. At the dashed `C=1` line, they are still relatively small, keeping probabilities close to 0.5.

The penalty does not know which words represent useful evidence. It shrinks suspicious features such as `the` and `for` as well as more obvious sentiment words such as `not` and `broke`. Regularisation limits how strongly the model trusts its training examples; it does not prove that a learned pattern is meaningful. The source uses more data to check that later.
