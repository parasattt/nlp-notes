The left plot shows training loss, and the right plot compares training and test accuracy. Both use a logarithmic step axis so early and late learning can be seen together.

```python
h = np.array(history)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.2))
ax[0].plot(h[:, 0], h[:, 1])
ax[0].set_xscale("log"); ax[0].set_xlabel("step"); ax[0].set_title("training loss")
ax[1].plot(h[:, 0], h[:, 2], label="train accuracy")
ax[1].plot(h[:, 0], h[:, 3], label="test accuracy")
ax[1].set_xscale("log"); ax[1].set_xlabel("step"); ax[1].set_ylim(0.4, 1.02)
ax[1].set_title("accuracy"); ax[1].legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Read the saved loss and accuracy plots together. Training loss keeps dropping, and training accuracy quickly approaches 1. Test accuracy stops improving early and stays around the same level.

The final saved step has training loss `0.0508`, training accuracy `1.00`, and test accuracy `0.57`. The model has 324 vocabulary weights but only 90 training reviews. Much of the later learning makes it more confident about examples it already knows, without improving predictions on the unseen reviews. This is **overfitting**.

The test set has only 30 reviews, so one changed prediction moves accuracy by `1 / 30 ≈ 3.3` percentage points. A shift from 0.57 to 0.60 is just one review. The later regularisation topic shows a standard way to limit large weights.
