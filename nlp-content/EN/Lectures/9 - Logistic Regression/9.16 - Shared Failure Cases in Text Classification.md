## Inspect examples every setup gets wrong

The next code asks a stricter question: which reviews are misclassified by **all five** model/representation setups?

Each setup makes out-of-fold predictions using the same five-fold split. `all_wrong` begins as `True` for every review and keeps only cases where every setup was wrong. The full saved list is retained so you can study the actual failure cases.



```python
all_wrong = np.ones(len(reviews), dtype=bool)
for pipe in setups.values():
    pred = cross_val_predict(pipe, reviews, y_all, cv=cv5)
    all_wrong &= (pred != y_all)

print(f"misclassified by all {len(setups)} setups: {all_wrong.sum()}")
print()
for i in np.where(all_wrong)[0]:
    print(f"  [{review_labels[i]}]  {reviews[i]}")
```

All five setups misclassify the same 31 reviews, more than a quarter of the corpus. Many seem straightforward to a person. The source separates two reasons:

### Too little evidence for the words

Examples include `Soft material and the stitching is neat`, `Leaks water every time I use it`, and `Far too heavy to carry around`. The relevant words, such as `stitching`, `leaks`, and `heavy`, were seen once or never in the training folds. The model cannot learn a reliable coefficient from so little evidence. The misspelling in `The batery easily lasts 2 days` causes a similar missing-word problem. More varied training data can help with this group.

### A representation that loses the needed meaning

Other cases need more than an isolated word count:

- **Negation:** `No complaints`, `No problems after 6 months`, and `Never had a problem` are positive despite negative-sounding words. `Not happy with this purchase at all` and `I would NOT recommend this to anyone` are negative despite `happy` and `recommend`. Separate word features do not express which words are being negated.
- **Contrast:** in `Delivery was fast but the product is USELESS`, the judgement after `but` matters. A bag of words ignores order and treats `fast` the same on either side.
- **Knowledge about actions:** `Returned it the next day.` and `Had to send it back TWICE, still faulty` require understanding what returning a product implies. The notebook presents these as cases without a strong enough isolated sentiment cue.
- **Signals removed before learning:** `I regret buying this 😞` includes an obvious negative emoji, but the default tokenizer throws it away.

The first group is mainly a data-coverage problem. The second is a representation problem: more of the same word counts only partly helps if the features do not keep the needed information. Bigrams recover some local order when the exact pair occurs often enough, but a bag of words still does not represent full word order or the scope of negation.
