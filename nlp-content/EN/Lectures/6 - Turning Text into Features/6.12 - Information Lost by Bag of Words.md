## What bag of words loses



Vectorize two sentences with the same word counts but different order. `np.array_equal` explicitly tests whether the rows match.

```python
order = ["dog bites man", "man bites dog"]

v = CountVectorizer()
M = v.fit_transform(order).toarray()

print(v.get_feature_names_out())
print(M)
print()
print("identical vectors:", np.array_equal(M[0], M[1]))
```


**Saved output:**

```text
['bites' 'dog' 'man']
[[1 1 1]
 [1 1 1]]

identical vectors: True
```



`dog bites man` and `man bites dog` mean different things, but both vectors contain one `bites`, one `dog` and one `man`. The lecture jokes that one is ordinary and the other is front-page news.

Bag of words discards word order, grammar and which noun is the subject. A model that only receives these vectors cannot recover those differences. Bigrams restore a small two-word window of order, rather than a full grammatical structure.

Why use bag of words? For many tasks, the words already provide enough evidence. The lecture uses spam containing `viagra`, `free` and `click` as an example where grammar may add little. Bag of words is fast, understandable, works with relatively little data and lets you inspect which words affected a prediction. [[EN/Lectures/7 - Naive Bayes Classification/7.10 - Inspecting Learned Evidence and Dataset Bias|The Naive Bayes lecture]] uses that transparency.

For tasks where order and “who did what to whom” matter, such as translation and question answering, this limitation motivates the later neural models discussed by the course.
