## min_df and max_df: filter by document frequency

Large corpora can produce huge vocabularies containing typos, names used once and serial numbers. **Document frequency** counts the number of documents containing a word, regardless of how often it repeats inside one document.

The lecture motivates removing words seen in only one document because they may not help on other documents and still add columns. The following example also shows why a rare word can carry useful information.



Start with all 14 features from the four-review corpus so you can see both meaningful rare words and the typo `xqzzy`.

```python
corpus = [
    "delivery was fast and the phone works well",
    "delivery was slow but the phone works",
    "the phone works well and delivery was fast",
    "terrible phone xqzzy broke immediately",
]

base = CountVectorizer()
base.fit(corpus)
print("no trimming :", len(base.get_feature_names_out()), "features")
print(base.get_feature_names_out())
```


**Saved output:**

```text
no trimming : 14 features
['and' 'broke' 'but' 'delivery' 'fast' 'immediately' 'phone' 'slow'
 'terrible' 'the' 'was' 'well' 'works' 'xqzzy']
```



Require occurrence in at least two documents and compare the surviving vocabulary.

```python
trim = CountVectorizer(min_df=2)
trim.fit(corpus)
print("min_df=2    :", len(trim.get_feature_names_out()), "features")
print(trim.get_feature_names_out())
```


**Saved output:**

```text
min_df=2    : 8 features
['and' 'delivery' 'fast' 'phone' 'the' 'was' 'well' 'works']
```



`min_df=2` keeps a word only if it occurs in at least **two documents**. It cuts this vocabulary from 14 words to 8 and removes the typo `xqzzy`.

It also removes `terrible`, which occurs in only one document but is the clearest sentiment clue in the example. With only four documents, the threshold is too strong. On 40,000 documents, requiring two occurrences would be a much smaller restriction.

Interpret `min_df` in relation to the corpus size. Inspect which words it removes before copying a threshold from another example.



Raise the integer document-frequency threshold through 1, 2 and 3. Track both the feature count and whether `terrible` remains.

```python
for m in [1, 2, 3]:
    v = CountVectorizer(min_df=m)
    v.fit(corpus)
    kept = v.get_feature_names_out()
    print(f"min_df={m}: {len(kept):2} features | 'terrible' kept: {'terrible' in kept}")
```


**Saved output:**

```text
min_df=1: 14 features | 'terrible' kept: True
min_df=2:  8 features | 'terrible' kept: False
min_df=3:  5 features | 'terrible' kept: False
```



`max_df` removes words with document frequency **above** a chosen limit. With `max_df=0.9`, words present in more than 90% of documents are dropped.

This builds a stop word filter from your own corpus rather than using a general English list. The lecture presents it as a usually safer alternative to the fixed list discussed earlier, while it still requires checking what information disappears.
